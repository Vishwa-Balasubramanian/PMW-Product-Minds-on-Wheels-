⏺ 1. App, pipeline, or both?

  Both: a pipeline inside an app. The two parts do different jobs.

  - The pipeline is the engine. It reads 13,000 comments, groups them into
    themes, checks them against the surveys and price lists, and drafts
    requirements. That's slow and costly, so it runs once in the background
    before the demo and saves its results.
  - The app is what the judges see and click. The product manager reviews the
    drafted requirements there: approves, rejects, edits, challenges, adjusts
    priority weights. Those actions happen live.

  The brief asks for "an AI-powered application" and a "demonstrable prototype",
  so the app is required. But one of the 4 deliverables is the workflow (what
  the AI does alone vs. where a human must step in), and the pipeline is that
  workflow. You need both, and the app should show the pipeline's stages so
  judges can see how a requirement came to be.

  2. Web app or desktop app?

  Web app. No real contest:
  - The demo is easier. You open a URL on any laptop or projector, and judges
    can try it on their phones.
  - It's faster to build. Next.js plus Vercel deploys in minutes. A desktop app
    means packaging, installers and OS issues, with no benefit.
  - It's realistic. Product managers at BMW work in browsers with teammates, and
    an audit trail of "who approved what" only makes sense in a shared tool.
  - Submission asks for a GitHub repo and a live demo, and a hosted URL covers
    both.

❯ explain the 7 step pipeline in a simple way                                   

⏺ I'll follow one real example through all 7 steps: customers struggling with
  the climate controls.

  1. Ingest (read everything)
  The AI loads all the files: comments, surveys, sales numbers, price lists. It
  fixes small problems, like numbers stored as text and one comment split across
  several rows.
  → 4,000 5 Series comments, ready to use.

  2. Signals (find what customers keep saying)
  The AI groups similar comments into themes, like sorting a huge pile of
  letters into stacks.
  → "Climate controls are hard to reach": 45 comments, several marked "serious".

  3. Triangulate (check whether other sources agree)
  It looks for the same problem elsewhere: the survey, the price list, the web.
  → The survey agrees (20% unhappy with climate controls). The web shows 
  competitors use physical buttons. Three sources agree, so evidence is strong.

  4. Derive (write the suggestion)
  The AI turns the problem into a clear suggestion for the next car.
  → "REQ-012: Give one-touch access to climate and frequently used functions."

  5. Score (decide how important it is)
  A simple formula scores it: how many customers, how upset they are, how much
  the car sells, what competitors do. The product manager can change the
  weights.
  → Score 82/100, ranked #3, with the reason shown.

  6. PM review (the human decides)
  The product manager opens the card, reads the real quotes and numbers, then
  approves, rejects, edits, or challenges it ("show me what contradicts this").
  → Approved, with a note: "Focus on climate first."

  7. Audit (record everything)
  Every step is logged: what the AI did, what the product manager changed, when,
  and why.
  → Anyone can trace REQ-012 back to the exact 45 customer comments it came 
  from.

  In short: steps 1–5 are the AI doing the heavy reading and drafting, step 6 is
  the human making the decision, and step 7 keeps a record of it all.

  What's built

  The pipeline (Python) turns your 8 BMW files into one SQLite database in about
  2 seconds:
  - Ingest: reads all the files and records each data problem it finds, e.g. the
    broken sales formula, 11–14% of comments with no topic label, no China
    comments, Germany-only price lists.
  - Signals: groups comments into themes. A scope guard removes out-of-scope
    topics with a reason; auto start-stop, for example, is excluded as a
    regulatory topic.
  - Cross-check: matches each theme to the survey questions, and computes
    missing voices and China-vs-Europe conflicts. For the 7 Series these include
    rear roominess 7.52 vs 9.41 and braking 7.82 vs 9.60.
  - Scoring: priority and confidence are calculated separately, by code, and
    place each requirement in Act / Validate first / Monitor / Park.
  - Challenge: each requirement gets a red-team check computed from the data
    itself.

  The web app (Next.js, bmw.de look, real car photos from the PDFs):
  - Home: three full-height photo panels, one per car, with a 1-second entrance.
    Each shows live numbers for your region. The 1 Series is greyed out in the

    comments are hidden, and that access gets logged.
  - 7 Series page with 4 tabs:

      ladder, priority and confidence with every factor explained, real quotes
      with their IDs, survey data, assumptions, Approve / Validate first / Edit
      / Reject / Challenge, and that requirement's audit trail.
    - Decision matrix
    - Signals
    - Data health: missing voices, market conflicts, and every data problem with
